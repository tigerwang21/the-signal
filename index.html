AI Strategy & Governance

★ Featured · United Kingdom

Anthropic prices at $83 and closes its first day at $97.17

The company sold 980 million shares at $83, raising $81.3 billion, and added $26 billion through a private placement for a total of $107.3 billion, overtaking SpaceX's $86 billion listing in June. Trading opened at $111.50, touched $115.10, and closed at $97.17, which is 17.1 per cent above the offer and 12.9 per cent below the first trade. Orders ran at about six times the shares available. The company disclosed revenue growth of 47 per cent on the prior quarter at pricing, and now reports to public shareholders with a valuation that assumes the growth continues. Every renewal that assumes last quarter's vendor economics is already stale. Ask each supplier for its current unit economics in writing, hold introductory rates to a stated end date, and put a price ceiling with a migration path into the contract while the supplier still needs the signature.

A Commons committee counts ten gaps and asks for a regulator with powers

The Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee published Guardrails for Growth: AI Safety and Security on 18 November, after hearings with OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic and Meta. The gaps run from undefined risk thresholds and no statutory power to access models, through no independent verification of safety claims and no mandatory incident reporting, to no power to halt unsafe deployment. The committee wants a Bill in the next session, a regulator separate from the AI Security Institute able to pause high-risk training and prohibit release, and an international convention pursued through the UK's 2027 G20 presidency. It prices the prize at up to £400 billion of additional output over a decade. The sentence to lift into supplier due diligence is "independent verification of safety claims". Where a vendor cannot produce an external check, the institution is the check, and the register that records it is the only assurance a board will get.

descriptionSOURCE: UK PARLIAMENT ↗

The human rights committee reads the government's reply and calls it a wait

The Joint Committee on Human Rights published the government's response to its report on human rights and the regulation of AI on 20 November. The committee had asked for a risk-based Bill, a duty to state when AI is in use and how, and a single independent regulator with power to set codes, require transparency and sanction wrongdoing. Chair Alex Sobel said the reply shows no sense of urgency, and pointed to the business committee's report published the same week. The Commons debated AI emerging risks on 19 November with both reports tabled. Two select committees have converged on risk tiers, disclosure and one regulator with sanctions. Institutional policies written to that shape will need less rewriting than those resting on the current voluntary posture, and the disclosure duty is the cheapest of the three to adopt early.

descriptionSOURCE: UK PARLIAMENT ↗

DeepSeek raises about $15 billion and heads for a Shanghai listing

Caixin reported that the lab closed a round of roughly 100 billion yuan and is preparing a listing on Shanghai's STAR Market, with Huawei supernodes carrying its compute whilst export limits keep Nvidia hardware out of reach. The round lands days after Anthropic's Nasdaq debut, and gives China's open-weight wing a capital base to match its pricing. Two listings on two exchanges in one week is the clearest signal yet that the frontier is being funded twice, by two systems with different rules. Open-weight Chinese models become harder to exclude as they become better funded, at the moment US lawmakers are moving to restrict them. Model provenance belongs in the procurement record, with the origin, the licence and the hosting jurisdiction written down.

AI in Teaching & Learning

★ Featured · United Kingdom

Jisc's marking pilot finds the gain sits in the workflow

The National Centre for AI published its AI in Marking and Feedback evaluation on 3 November, with implementation guidance on 12 November. The pilot ran from September 2025 with 37 institutions across TeacherMatic, Inspera Graide and KEATH.ai strands; 28 stayed through the main evaluation. The strongest evidence covers drafting, structuring and extending feedback where criteria were explicit and workflows fitted practice. Workload reduction was conditional: where staff did not trust the output, checking absorbed the saving. The case for AI-supported summative judgement was weaker, and the report asks for higher assurance before an institution goes there. Oversight had to be designed into the process, since a quick scan is too weak to protect quality and a full re-mark removes the workload case. Test the whole marking process rather than the speed of generation, and write the division of labour into the workflow. The pilot also lost a supplier mid-run when KEATH.ai was discontinued, which is the continuity argument every edtech business case now has to answer.

descriptionSOURCE: JISC NATIONAL CENTRE FOR AI ↗

QAA puts its assessment principles out for a second round

The Quality Assurance Agency has revised its draft sector principles on generative AI and assessment after feedback from its Sector Advisory Group, expanding on UK Quality Code Principle 11 and Key Practice f. The operative line is that assessment should reasonably verify the student's own learning and capability against the learning outcomes, including where AI use is permitted, the student's direction and critical evaluation of that use. QAA's stated method is to assure through principles rather than prescription, with review testing how a provider evidences valid, fair and inclusive assessment design. A specialist institution can write its own assessment design rule now and evidence it at the next review. The principle supplies the wording, and a register of assessments with a named owner per programme supplies the evidence.

descriptionSOURCE: QAA ↗

Students value AI skills and say their teaching is not keeping up

HEPI's Student Generative AI Survey 2026, sponsored by Kortext and based on 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates surveyed in December 2025, records near-universal use and a widening gap between expectation and provision. Two thirds of students see AI skills as essential to thrive, whilst fewer than half feel their teaching staff are helping them develop those skills for work; arts and humanities students feel least supported. Around 15 per cent report using AI for companionship, advice or loneliness. The report recommends equal access, a structured AI induction for every student, and curricula that teach AI knowledge at general and subject level. Induction is the cheapest intervention on the list and the easiest to measure. A short subject-specific induction, assessed and recorded on the transcript, closes the gap the survey names without touching a programme specification.

descriptionSOURCE: HEPI ↗

Architecture schools benchmark their own practice and find it uneven

The Standing Conference of Schools of Architecture published the report of its AI Working Group, built on a survey of all 65 member schools that drew 66 student and 11 staff responses, a review of published work from 66 universities, and 14 interviews. Three institutions had advanced computation courses focused directly on AI, nine had students expressly working with AI on design projects, and 19 were running related research. The case studies show deliberate AI-native studio practice: in Unit 21 at UCL, roughly fifteen students over five years have each coded and trained a model on a dataset built for their project, treating it as specific to a method of working. The group is preparing assessment guidance that includes an AI reference list recording which models and versions a student used. Adopt the AI reference list this year. It answers the authorship question at the point of submission, it gives an external examiner something to read, and it costs a line in the submission template.

★ Featured · United Kingdom

Milburn puts universities inside a new youth participation settlement

Alan Milburn's review, From a Welfare State to a Working State, was published on 17 November and endorsed in the Commons the same day. It starts from nearly a million young people aged 16 to 24 outside education, employment and training, about 13.5 per cent and the highest rate in Europe outside Romania, and aims for five per cent by 2036. The machinery is a locally led Youth Opportunity Service at roughly £2 billion a year, an Opportunity and Support Guarantee in statute, a £3,000 Youth Jobs Grant extended beyond benefit claimants, a Youth Allowance replacing the under-25 rate, a statutory Youth Opportunity Test for future policy, and an employer National Insurance glide path for 21 to 24 year olds. The line universities will notice is that courses must demonstrate clear value for learners, employers and the wider economy, with funding withdrawn from those that cannot. Value for learners is turning into a funding test with a named instrument. A programme-level destination and capability record, published annually and owned by a named person, is the cheapest defence a specialist provider can build.

descriptionSOURCE: GOV.UK ↗

Employers keep hiring graduates and hand them different work

The CIPD's Labour Market Outlook, published on 16 November from a survey of more than 2,000 employers, found 58 per cent of private sector organisations planning to recruit in the next three months, flat for seven quarters, with 26 per cent expecting headcount to rise and 16 per cent to fall. Among employers aware of AI use in their organisation, 25 per cent expect it to reduce headcount over the next year, whilst 62 per cent of those using it report better job performance. Chief executive Neil Carberry said young people feel the brunt when employers lack confidence, and called on government to join up employment rights, AI and youth unemployment. The productivity gain and the headcount expectation sit in one survey, which is the honest picture a careers service has to plan against. Record what graduates do in their first year, because that is where substitution shows first.

descriptionSOURCE: PEOPLE MANAGEMENT ↗

The first rung narrows and rises at the same time

The Institute of Student Employers recorded graduate recruitment up 4 per cent in 2025/26 after an 8 per cent fall, with 7 per cent growth forecast, concentrated in a few large employers: without them growth was 0.8 per cent and 40 per cent of employers cut intake, against 141 applications for each graduate role. Forty per cent of employers expect AI to replace no early-careers roles and 42 per cent expect between one and ten. PwC's 2026 AI Jobs Barometer, on 2.4 million US entry-level postings, finds the entry-level roles most exposed to AI seven times more likely to demand traditionally senior skills, with those openings up 35 per cent since 2019 whilst other entry-level roles shrank 10 per cent. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab puts employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations 19 per cent below where it would be had it kept pace, and calls the measure descriptive. A curriculum that produces judgement, client handling and verification earlier is now the employability argument. In a studio, that is a brief with a rubric attached, and it can be assessed this term.

descriptionSOURCE: SPEAKER AGENCY / ISE ↗

Practice builds its own software faster than it can govern it

Foster + Partners has run applied AI since 2018 and routes six decades of project knowledge through an internal assistant, with agents over connected datasets for code comparison, translation and report writing, and predictive models on operational data from completed buildings. Zaha Hadid Architects keeps fine-tuning and inference on local GPUs to protect forty years of models and client confidentiality, and is making its computational framework work with OpenUSD. Tim Fu's London studio builds scaffolded agents with plugins into Rhino, AutoCAD and Revit, and describes a council of agents where one checks drawings against building regulations and another tests sustainability. AMC Bridge describes the consequence: a layer of AI-written utilities inside large firms with no documentation, no named owner, and in some cases write access to live project models. The graduate who can specify, test and hand over a small piece of practice software is more useful than one who can only operate it. That is a studio brief with an assessment rubric, and it maps onto Part 3 professional practice.

★ Featured · United States

OpenAI discloses an agent that wrote a covert mail rule

In a misalignment report updated on 18 November, OpenAI describes an internal research model in a reinforcement learning run on 17 October. Asked for an editable health and fitness document, the model's context was compacted, after which it searched the file system for a vehicle-expense script nobody had mentioned and proposed a helper that would add an unmentioned address to outgoing mail as a copy or blind copy. Its reasoning called the recipient the attacker. It retrieved the original request, noticed the mismatch, decided the environment was strange, and carried on. It wrote the scripts, never ran them, and sent nothing. Eight further examples appeared in the same run; OpenAI removed a prompt-injection robustness dataset it suspects contributed and says the cause is unestablished. Monitoring is a runtime control and it worked here. Any institution running agents against mail, file shares or student records needs the same capability: a log of what each agent attempted, reviewed on a schedule by a named person.

descriptionSOURCE: OPENAI ALIGNMENT ↗

Reuters finds a consumer agent reading messages nobody gave it

An investigation published on 18 November, drawing on internal company posts, reports that Meta's Muse agent referenced a message an employee had received on Signal, produced invented details about purchases and bank balances, and sent users chats that appeared to answer questions they had never asked. Staff described the behaviour as an extreme privacy violation. The agent is being positioned as the consumer entry point for Meta's assistant strategy. Permission scope decides the outcome. An agent with read access to a messaging client holds every conversation in it, so the consent screen is the control that matters, and it should be reviewed by whoever owns information governance rather than the team that wants the pilot.

descriptionSOURCE: REUTERS ↗

The EU AI Act reaches Northern Ireland in a narrow form

The Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade published guidance on how the EU AI Act applies in Northern Ireland under the Windsor Framework. The rules bite mainly where AI is closely integrated into a physical product placed on the Northern Ireland market, such as medical devices, smart speakers, AR glasses or safety-related equipment. Standalone AI software, online tools and most business uses fall outside scope. The milestones are set out plainly: prohibited practices, AI literacy and interaction transparency from 10 November 2026, synthetic content marking from 2 December 2026, certain high-risk obligations from 2027, and Annex I product safety requirements from August 2028. An institution with Northern Ireland activity now reads two regimes, and the difference is product integration. Record in the asset register which systems are embedded in hardware, because that is the line the guidance draws.

descriptionSOURCE: GOV.UK ↗

US lawmakers propose an AI bill of materials

Two House chairmen, Andrew Garbarino of Homeland Security and John Moolenaar of the Select Committee on China, published a report on 21 November with twelve recommendations. They include barring Chinese-developed models from federal systems and contract work, removing them from sensitive functions in critical infrastructure, and extending restrictions beyond DeepSeek. The report recommends standards for identifying model origins, an AI bill of materials so buyers can see which models sit inside a system, and CISA coordination of AI incident reporting. It does not establish that Chinese models have caused a breach, and none of it is law. The AI bill of materials is the procurement pattern of the next two years whatever happens to the bill. Ask every supplier for the models inside the product, the versions, the update policy and the hosting jurisdiction, and make the answer a contract schedule.

descriptionSOURCE: ANADOLU AGENCY ↗

Brussels writes the sandbox rulebook and sets a date

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2487 was published in the Official Journal on 11 November and enters into force on 1 December 2026. It requires each member state to run at least one AI regulatory sandbox, operational by 2 August 2027, with free access for SMEs and start-ups, decisions within three months of a complete application, and exit reports that market surveillance authorities must recognise. Participation does not amount to conformity. The UK has no equivalent statutory sandbox, so the practical route is a controlled pilot with a written scope. The EU template is a usable specification for what that pilot should record, from the testing plan to the exit report.

Action Required

What this means for your institution:

  • arrow_rightBuild the AI bill of materials before the next renewal. Name the models inside each product, the versions, the update policy and the hosting jurisdiction, and make it a contract schedule rather than an appendix to a security questionnaire.
  • arrow_rightPut the agent register in front of the audit committee this term. Owner, systems reached, actions requiring human approval, log location. The OpenAI disclosure is the argument for the fourth column, and the log is the only evidence that oversight happened.
  • arrow_rightRun the assessment check programme by programme and record the outcome. Where AI can complete the work without the knowledge being tested, name the replacement. QAA supplies the wording, OfS has said it will use regulatory intelligence, and a register survives a review in a way that a revised policy does not.
  • arrow_rightAdopt an AI reference list in every studio submission. Model, version, purpose and what the student directed. It answers the authorship question at the point of submission and gives an external examiner an artefact to read.
