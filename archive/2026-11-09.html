★ Featured · United Kingdom

Seven principles put control and sovereignty above model choice

The Financial Times reported on 5 November that Andy Burnham and his senior team approved a framework governing domestic AI adoption and the infrastructure build-out, after what the paper describes as candid exchanges in cabinet. Seven core principles were signed off, including that AI and its adoption must work in the interests of British people, that control and sovereignty matter, and that infrastructure must serve a national purpose and deliver for communities. The framework follows a Budget that funded an AI biosecurity lab and a data centre covenant. None of the seven principles is legislation, and the useful question for a provider is what each one asks of a contract. Put three questions to every AI renewal this term: where the inference runs, who can withdraw the model, and what the institution takes away at exit. A supplier that answers in writing has given you a schedule you can audit; one that will not has given the board a finding before the money is committed.

The Budget funds the security half of the AI story

Hogan Lovells' reading of the Budget, published on 3 November, puts more than £500 million behind secure foundations for an AI-enabled economy, with the weight of it on safety and security. The Chancellor also expanded the Sovereign AI Fund and set out local AI partnerships, alongside an AI Biosecurity Lab and a data centre covenant. The NCSC release of the same week adds £60 million for a first-phase national Cyber Shield, £70 million for a National Exposure and Real-Time Vulnerability Centre, and an AI Cyber Security Centre of Excellence in Manchester. Money of this shape tends to arrive as programmes with eligibility criteria attached. Map the institution's roadmap against the funded programmes now, because the bid windows will be short and the security line is where a small provider can win resource it could never justify from its own capital budget.

descriptionSOURCE: HOGAN LOVELLS VIA JD SUPRA ↗

Anthropic tells investors it may be close to recursive self-improvement

An amended S-1 filed on 2 November states that the company may imminently meet its own Responsible Scaling Policy threshold for dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI, the condition it labels recursive self-improvement. The filing stops short of claiming that a deployed Claude model has crossed the line. The same document discloses third-quarter revenue near $17 billion against $895.4 billion in compute commitments. Whatever a reader makes of the claim, the disclosure has a practical edge for institutions: capability thresholds now sit inside vendor risk documents that a procurement team can read and cite. Treat model discontinuity as a standing risk. Any teaching or research workflow that depends on a named model version needs that version written into the agreement, with a notice period and a tested fallback, because the supplier's own filings now say the ground may move without much warning.

descriptionSOURCE: TECHCRUNCH ↗

Alphabet sets 2027 capital spending above $300 billion

Alphabet reported third-quarter revenue of $127.6 billion with Google Cloud up 103 per cent to $30.7 billion, and guided 2027 capital expenditure above $300 billion. Microsoft disclosed $10 billion of revenue from OpenAI in the quarter, about 11 per cent of its total. OpenAI trimmed its current round to $20 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation, according to the Financial Times, and Microsoft declined to renew a server rental contract with Oracle because the hardware carried older chips. The companies absorbing this spending will recover it through price. Assume per-seat and per-token prices rise at renewal, and model a 30 per cent uplift across the three largest AI lines in the next budget round. A multi-year agreement without a price ceiling transfers the whole of that risk to the institution.

descriptionSOURCE: AI WEEKLY ↗

Brussels moves the high-risk clock to December 2027

The Digital Omnibus on AI, now agreed between the EU institutions, defers high-risk obligations for stand-alone systems in Annex III, a list that includes education and employment use cases, from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. Transparency duties largely stay where they were, and conformity assessment, technical documentation and human oversight requirements remain on the statute book. The deferral reflects unreadiness among national authorities and missing harmonised standards. Institutions with EU partners, franchise arrangements or research consortia inherit these duties through the partnership even where UK law is silent. A due diligence questionnaire that asks a vendor for its Annex III classification takes an afternoon and settles the question before a validation panel asks it.

AI in Teaching & Learning

★ Featured · United Kingdom

Jisc hands leaders three pillars and a maturity model

Jisc published its AI strategic framework for colleges and universities on 3 November, built with members around three pillars: skills, knowledge and culture; technology; and governance. Beneath each pillar sits a set of considerations intended to help a leadership team set boundaries, commit resource and place accountability. A companion maturity model frames where an institution sits and what a move to the next stage requires. The value of this pair is procedural: it gives an AI committee an agenda that survives a change of chair. Adopt the three pillars as the standing agenda for the institutional AI group, and use the maturity model to record a baseline this term. A recorded baseline is what turns next year's progress claim into evidence, and it costs two meetings.

descriptionSOURCE: JISC ↗

Kent opens ChatGPT Edu to everyone and attaches a test to the key

The University of Kent published its position on 6 November: ChatGPT Edu is free to all students and staff, the institution does not mandate its use, and tutors decide how AI applies within their own modules. Access follows a short module on using the tool, granted within 24 hours of completion. Staff cannot see student prompts, though limited technical staff can reach data for legal reasons under senior management authority. Two open events, an AI town hall on 11 November and a live session on 18 November, carry the explanation. The onboarding module is the control worth copying. It converts a licence into a record of who has been told what, which is the artefact an institution produces when a student appeal or a regulator asks how the tool was introduced.

descriptionSOURCE: UNIVERSITY OF KENT ↗

Liverpool writes the AI tier into the assessment record

Liverpool's guidance, published for students on 3 November and in force since August, places every assessment in one of four tiers: no AI including grammar checkers, limited contextual support with a declaration, critical engagement where drafts are critiqued and cited, and AI integral to the task. The tier is set within the assessment brief, visible before the work begins, and the institution provides a secure gateway to approved models for the tiers that require them. The scheme turns a judgement about intent into a published rule recorded against a named assessment. A tier held in the curriculum record is the cheapest control available to an institution facing an OfS review or a student appeal, because it answers the question a panel actually asks: what did the brief permit, and where was that written down.

descriptionSOURCE: UNIVERSITY OF LIVERPOOL ↗

A £30 million national lab bets on open models that run on ordinary hardware

The Science of Fundamental AI Research lab, led by David Barber at UCL with Cambridge, Oxford and Edinburgh, was profiled by Science and Engineering South on 5 November. The £30 million lab is part of a £60 million EPSRC scheme to rethink how AI systems are built, with the explicit aim of making them efficient enough to run on widely available hardware and less dependent on a handful of global providers. Barber frames the work as fundamental research into new architectures and training methods, drawing on mathematics, statistics and neuroscience. Research computing sovereignty gets a domestic supplier of ideas and, in time, of models. An institution that can run a credible open-weight model on its own estate holds a negotiating position that no licence agreement confers.

descriptionSOURCE: SCIENCE AND ENGINEERING SOUTH ↗

QAA's numbers explain why consistency beats policy volume

QAA's State of the Nation report on AI and assessment records that the share of students saying their assessment has changed in response to generative AI rose from 32 per cent in the 2024 HEPI/Kortext survey to 65 per cent in 2026, and that AI featured in more than half of external QAA reviews in 2025 against roughly one in ten in 2023. The finding that matters is internal: variance runs through departments, programmes, modules and individual tutors inside a single institution. QAA is responding with a community of practice, a refresh of the Academic Integrity Charter and training as a cross-cutting theme of its 2026-27 membership offer. Audit variance before a reviewer does. Sample three modules in the same programme, compare the AI rules students actually received, and fix the divergence. The exposure is a student on the same award getting a different rule from the one published.

AI Workforce & Skills

03
★ Featured · United Kingdom

PwC finds the first rung has been raised

The 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer, built on 2.4 million US entry-level postings, finds that entry-level roles most exposed to AI are seven times more likely to require traditionally senior capabilities such as motivational leadership, strategic decision-making and team building, which now account for 52 per cent of new skills demanded in those roles against 7 per cent in the least exposed. Those seniorised entry-level jobs have grown 35 per cent since 2019 whilst other entry-level roles shrank 10 per cent, and globally the count of AI-exposed entry-level jobs has flatlined. Headcount at the most AI-exposed organisations grew twice as fast as at the least exposed. The junior task that trained a graduate has moved up the ladder. Where a programme still assesses the routine artefact, write an assessed substitute that requires judgement under unfamiliar conditions, because that is the work employers now advertise at entry level.

descriptionSOURCE: PWC ↗

Salford builds governance and judgement into an AI apprenticeship

The University of Salford opened applications on 4 November for an AI and Automation Practitioner Apprenticeship, run between its business school and computer science staff, with an employer webinar on 23 November. The standard asks apprentices to identify where AI delivers value, weigh the governance and ethical context, and implement solutions with low-code and no-code tools, with no prior AI knowledge required. Salford positions it for public sector, regulated and high-stakes employers where the cost of getting AI wrong is high. Apprenticeship standards are where assessment design meets employer demand with money attached. A specialist institution can shape a standard through a trailblazer group at far lower cost than it can reshape a degree, and the standard then constrains what every provider must teach.

descriptionSOURCE: UNIVERSITY OF SALFORD ↗

A US survey finds a quarter of AI-using employers hiring fewer juniors

Northeastern University's AI and Society Survey, reported on 2 November, questioned 8,174 people with a say in hiring. Roughly half said AI had changed their decisions; of those, 26 per cent reported hiring fewer entry-level candidates and 7 per cent more, whilst 28 per cent expected greater output or higher-level work from the juniors they do hire. Around 38 per cent said a degree makes a candidate more valuable, with about one in five calling it much more valuable. Almost half said AI experience was not explicitly on their radar. Employers are paying for judgement and asking juniors to produce more of it sooner. That is an argument for the degree, provided the programme can show where judgement was formed and tested, which is a transcript and portfolio question.

descriptionSOURCE: NORTHEASTERN UNIVERSITY ↗

Recruiters and business schools agree on the skills and split on readiness

GMAC's second Corporate Recruiters Pulse Survey, reported on 4 November, asked 89 US recruiters and 61 programme leaders to rate 24 AI skills. Both groups put the same four at the top: synthesising large volumes of information, understanding AI's limitations and risks, using AI ethically and responsibly, and analysing data and interpreting output. They diverge on readiness, with 36 per cent of recruiters judging graduates adequately or very well prepared against 17 per cent of programme leaders. One in three employers reported already replacing at least some entry-level roles with AI. The gap between what employers rank first and what they think graduates can do is the assurance argument for an AI literacy strand with assessment attached. A graduate attribute statement without an assessed artefact behind it will not close that gap in a recruiter's mind.

descriptionSOURCE: POETS AND QUANTS ↗

RIBA counts 74 per cent practice adoption and a worried pipeline

RIBA's 2026 AI report, published in July and still the sector's best denominator, records that practices using AI on most projects rose from 59 per cent in 2025 to 74 per cent in 2026, with 75 per cent of users reporting a productivity gain and 57 per cent a positive return. Against that, 59 per cent expect AI to reduce staff across the profession, 61 per cent say it will make it harder for early-career professionals to acquire essential skills, and 17 per cent agree their designs are better because of it. Martha Tsigkari of Foster + Partners told an Architects' Journal session in September that the practice has run AI since 2018, and Patrik Schumacher of Zaha Hadid Architects calls it an unmitigated boon. RIBA is preparing an AI overlay to the Plan of Work and a responsible-use policy. Studio teaching carries the risk the survey identifies. A student who prompts a massing study has not practised the judgement the practice will test in year one. Put a defended decision into every studio brief, with the model outputs kept as evidence and the reasoning assessed.

★ Featured · United Kingdom

The Bill gives ministers four levers over agents that run loose

A factsheet updated on 3 November sets out the agentic AI measures intended under the Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill: security requirements for operators of essential and digital services, notification of affected organisations, preservation of evidence, and government-led incident reviews after an incident in which an operator could not adequately monitor or control an AI agent. The powers would come through changes to the NIS Regulations after consultation, and ministers describe them with a narrow scope tied to loss of control. The Register reported on 6 November that officials frame the measures as a route to national understanding of agentic risk. Build the artefact an inspector will ask for: a register of agents that can write, with a named owner, the systems each reaches, the actions requiring human approval, and the log it writes to. That register answers four statutory questions in an hour and costs a fortnight to assemble.

descriptionSOURCE: GOV.UK ↗

The cyber chief gives the sector two years

The NCSC's State of the Cyber Nation, published on 3 November, judges it highly likely that AI-powered malware will have been used in a ransomware attack on UK critical national infrastructure between now and 2028, and assesses that Russia and China will routinely use AI to pursue geopolitical goals by then. Richard Horne told the launch that the era of tech on the cheap is over, and that the risk arises because long-tolerated vulnerabilities can now be found and exploited at a scale that was once unimaginable. The report names abuse of an open-source AI red-teaming platform in attacks on UK organisations, and contemplates an autonomous attacker operating beyond its operator's control. The estate an institution has neglected is the estate an agent will find. Patch discipline, network exposure and a tested restore belong in the same plan as the AI roadmap, because the regulator's timeline now runs to 2028 and the audit trail starts before the incident.

descriptionSOURCE: NCSC ↗

Data centres become an election issue and reach the campus gate

Democratic gains in the US midterms included victories in Texas, Georgia and Ohio on pledges to curb data centre construction, according to the Financial Times, and Texas's governor-elect said the state will legislate to shield residents from data centres. OpenAI opened a public campaign to change voters' views on the facilities, Google bought a Wyoming site from Crusoe, and Microsoft ended a server rental contract with Oracle over older chips. Planning consent for compute is now an electoral question in the country where most of the models institutions use are trained. Any hosting arrangement, land deal or research partnership that carries a data centre now carries a local political risk. Ask for the consultation record before signing, and expect a student union or a residents' group to read it back at you.

descriptionSOURCE: REUTERS ↗

Beijing writes a full-lifecycle law and asks for incident disclosure

The National Development and Reform Commission said on 3 November that it will accelerate a comprehensive AI law covering data, computing power, algorithms and deployment, with safety and ethical standards applying from training through operation, periodic compliance assessment and mandatory disclosure of incidents and vulnerabilities. The scope named DeepSeek, Moonshot and Alibaba's Qwen family. Premier Li Qiang used the Wuzhen summit to stress balance between innovation and security, and researchers from DeepSeek, Qwen and MiniMax conceded on a panel that open weights carry inherent risk. China has graded agent autonomy step by step since 15 July. Model provenance is becoming a research-integrity question. Where a project uses an open-weight model from a jurisdiction with new disclosure duties, record the version, the licence and the evaluation, because a collaborator's compliance obligation can travel with the weights.

descriptionSOURCE: FORKLOG ↗

The regulator's thirty questions close on 20 November

The ICO's call for evidence on agentic AI sets out thirty questions across security, transparency, accountability, automated decision-making and lawfulness, and closes on 20 November. The themes include agent identity, isolation between sessions, the logs that support oversight, whether an agent may reach internal knowledge systems, and how human approval is recorded for consequential actions. The regulator states plainly that policy alone cannot answer these questions and that governance and technical controls carry the weight, with accountability resting on the deployer. An unanswered call for evidence becomes someone else's code of practice. Where an agent touches student records or a supplier system, the controller analysis and the impact assessment belong to the university, and a data protection assessment written for a chatbot will not cover an agent that acts.
